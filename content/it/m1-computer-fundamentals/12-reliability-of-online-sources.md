## Lesson

Anyone can publish anything online. There is no editor, no fact-checker and no
requirement to be right. A page that is completely false looks exactly like a
page that is completely true — same fonts, same layout, same confident tone.

Judging which is which is therefore a skill, and the syllabus treats it as one.

### The questions to ask

**Who wrote it?** Is an author or organisation named? Are their qualifications
stated? Can you find out anything about them elsewhere? An anonymous page
cannot be held responsible for anything it says.

**Why was it published?** To inform, to teach, to sell, to persuade, or to
provoke? A page arguing that a supplement cures a disease, published by the
company selling it, is advertising. That does not make it false, but it does
mean it was not written to inform you.

**When was it written or last updated?** Information ages. Medical guidance,
prices, laws and statistics all go out of date, and a page with no date gives
you no way to tell.

**Where is the evidence?** Are sources cited? Do the links work and do they say
what the page claims? A page that cites nothing is asking to be taken on trust.

**Is it consistent with other sources?** If several independent sources agree,
confidence rises. If one page makes a claim nobody else makes, treat it with
suspicion until you find out why.

**How is it written?** Spelling mistakes, grammatical errors, shouting capitals
and extravagant claims all suggest carelessness. Careless presentation often
accompanies careless checking.

### The domain is a clue, not an answer

The end of a web address gives a hint about who runs the site:

| Domain | Usually |
|---|---|
| `.gov` | A government body |
| `.edu` | An educational institution |
| `.org` | A non-profit organisation |
| `.com` | A commercial business |
| `.net` | Originally networks; now anything |

`.gov` and `.edu` are restricted in most countries, so they carry real weight.
But `.org` is available to anyone, and a `.com` may be the most authoritative
source in its field — a manufacturer really is the best source on its own
product's specifications.

So use the domain as one signal among several, never as a decision on its own.

### Kinds of source, ranked roughly

| Source | Reliability |
|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed academic journal | Highest — checked by other experts |
| Government or official statistics | High |
| Established news organisation | Generally good; check for bias |
| University or educational site | Generally good |
| Reference works with named editors | Good |
| Wikipedia | Useful starting point; check its **sources** |
| Company website | Reliable about its own products; promotional otherwise |
| Personal blog | Depends entirely on the author |
| Social media post | Lowest — no checking of any kind |

**Wikipedia** deserves its own note, because students are told simply not to
use it. The useful advice is more precise: it is edited by volunteers and can
be wrong or vandalised, so it is not a source to cite. But its articles cite
their sources at the bottom, and **those** are often exactly what you need.
Use it to find sources, not as one.

### Bias

**Bias** is a slant towards one view. It is not the same as being wrong — a
biased page may contain only true statements and still mislead, by choosing
which true statements to include.

Signs to watch for: only one side presented; emotive language; claims with no
evidence; an obvious financial interest; opponents described dismissively.

The question is not "is this biased?" — almost everything is, to some degree —
but "does the bias affect what I am taking from it?"

### Fake news and misinformation

**Misinformation** is false information spread without intent to deceive, by
people who believe it.

**Disinformation** is false information spread deliberately, to mislead.

Both spread fastest when the content is surprising or makes the reader angry,
because that is what gets shared. The practical habit: **the more strongly a
story makes you feel, the more carefully you should check it** before
believing or passing it on.

Warning signs: a sensational headline that the article does not support; no
author; no date; no sources; an unfamiliar site imitating a known one; an image
that turns out to be old or from somewhere else.

### The CRAAP test

A widely used checklist, useful because it is memorable:

| Letter | Stands for | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | Currency | How recent is it? Is it still valid? |
| **R** | Relevance | Does it address what you need, at the right level? |
| **A** | Authority | Who wrote it, and what qualifies them? |
| **A** | Accuracy | Is it supported by evidence? Can it be verified? |
| **P** | Purpose | Why was it published? Is there bias? |

Any of these can rule a source out on its own.

### Practical habits

- **Check at least two independent sources** for anything important.
- **Follow the citation to its origin.** "Studies show" is not a source; find
  the study.
- **Check the date**, and check whether newer information exists.
- **Read past the headline**, which is often written to attract attention
  rather than to summarise.
- **Check the image**, which may be genuine but from another event or year.
- **Prefer the original** over a report of a report.
- **Notice your own agreement.** Information confirming what you already
  believe gets less scrutiny, from everyone.

### Why this matters in IT

Because the alternative is GIGO on a larger scale. Decisions, essays, projects
and businesses built on unreliable information fail in ways that trace directly
back to the source — and, as with data entry, the error is invisible once it is
inside the work.

## Flashcards

### 1.12 | Why must online information be judged more carefully than a printed textbook?
Anyone can publish anything online with no editor or fact-checker, and a false
page looks exactly like a true one.

### 1.12 | Name five questions to ask about an online source.
Who wrote it; why was it published; when was it written or updated; what
evidence is cited; does it agree with other independent sources.

### 1.12 | What does the CRAAP test stand for?
**Currency**, **Relevance**, **Authority**, **Accuracy**, **Purpose**.

### 1.12 | Why is a page with no date a concern?
Information ages — prices, laws, medical guidance and statistics all go out of
date — and without a date there is no way to judge whether it still holds.

### 1.12 | What does the domain of a website tell you?
A hint about who runs it: `.gov` government, `.edu` educational, `.org`
non-profit, `.com` commercial. It is one signal among several, not a decision
on its own.

### 1.12 | Why is `.org` weaker evidence of trustworthiness than `.gov`?
`.gov` and `.edu` are restricted in most countries, while `.org` is available
to anyone.

### 1.12 | How should Wikipedia be used?
As a starting point, not a source to cite. Its articles list their sources at
the bottom, and those are often what you actually need.

### 1.12 | What is bias, and how does it differ from being wrong?
A slant towards one view. A biased page may contain only true statements and
still mislead, by choosing which true statements to include.

### 1.12 | Distinguish between misinformation and disinformation.
**Misinformation** is false information spread without intent to deceive.
**Disinformation** is spread deliberately, to mislead.

### 1.12 | Why does surprising or angering content spread fastest?
Because that is what people share. The practical rule is that the more strongly
a story makes you feel, the more carefully you should check it.

> Hint: what does a headline get rewarded for?

### 1.12 | Name four warning signs of an unreliable article.
Any four: a sensational headline the article does not support; no named author;
no date; no sources cited; an unfamiliar site imitating a known one; spelling
and grammar errors; an image that is old or from elsewhere.

### 1.12 | Why is a company's page about its own product both reliable and unreliable?
It is the best source for the product's specifications, and a promotional one
for any claim about how good it is.

### 1.12 | Why does information confirming what you already believe deserve extra scrutiny?
Because everyone checks it less carefully, so it is where errors get through.

## Questions

### 1.12 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 1
Which is the strongest indicator that an online article is reliable?

- A. It appears first in the search results
- B. It names its author and cites sources that can be checked
- C. It has many images
- D. It has been shared widely on social media

**Answer:** B

**Working:** A named author can be held responsible and checkable sources can
be verified. Search ranking and sharing measure popularity, not accuracy.

### 1.12 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
What does the `A` for **Authority** in the CRAAP test ask?

- A. How recent the information is
- B. Who wrote it and what qualifies them
- C. Whether the page loads quickly
- D. Why it was published

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Authority concerns the author's identity and credentials.
Currency is recency and Purpose is why it was published.

### 1.12 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
A student finds a page arguing that a particular vitamin cures a disease. The
page belongs to the company that sells the vitamin. The student should

- A. accept it, since the company knows its own product
- B. treat it as promotional and check independent sources
- C. reject it because `.com` sites are never reliable
- D. accept it if the page looks professional

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The company has a financial interest in the claim, so its purpose
is to sell rather than to inform. It may be reliable about the product's
ingredients; the medical claim needs independent confirmation.

### 1.12 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Which statement about Wikipedia is most accurate?

- A. It should never be opened, as it is always wrong
- B. It is a good starting point, and its listed sources are often what should
  be used
- C. It is peer-reviewed by experts, so it can always be cited
- D. It is as reliable as a government statistics site

**Answer:** B

**Working:** It is edited by volunteers and can be wrong, so it is not a source
to cite — but its articles cite their own sources, which are frequently exactly
what a student needs.

### 1.12 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
False information spread deliberately in order to mislead is called

- A. misinformation
- B. disinformation
- C. bias
- D. plagiarism

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The difference is intent. Misinformation is spread by people who
believe it; disinformation is spread knowingly.

### 1.12 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Which domain gives the strongest indication that a site belongs to an official
body?

- A. `.com`
- B. `.org`
- C. `.gov`
- D. `.net`

**Answer:** C

**Working:** `.gov` is restricted to government bodies in most countries.
`.org` and `.net` may be registered by anyone, and `.com` is commercial.

### 1.12 | structured | 10 marks | difficulty 3
A student is researching the effects of climate change on Caribbean
agriculture, and finds three sources: a government meteorological service
report from last year, an undated blog post by an anonymous author, and an
article on a fertiliser company's website claiming its product prevents crop
loss.

(a) Name **four** criteria the student should use to judge each source.
**(4 marks)**

(b) For each source, state whether it is likely to be reliable, with a reason:

(i) the meteorological service report **(2 marks)**

(ii) the anonymous undated blog post **(2 marks)**

(iii) the fertiliser company's article **(2 marks)**

**Answer:** (a) 1 mark each, any four: currency — how recent it is; relevance —
whether it addresses the question; authority — who wrote it and what qualifies
them; accuracy — whether evidence and sources are given; purpose — why it was
published and whether there is bias. (Accept the CRAAP headings.)
(b)(i) 1 mark — likely reliable; 1 mark — it is from an official body with
expertise in the subject, it is recent, and such reports normally cite their
data.
(ii) 1 mark — unlikely to be reliable; 1 mark — no author is named, so the
writer's expertise cannot be judged and nobody is accountable, and with no date
there is no way to tell whether it is current.
(iii) 1 mark — treat with caution / partly reliable; 1 mark — the company has a
financial interest in the claim, so its purpose is promotional; it may be
accurate about its product's composition but the claim needs independent
confirmation.

**Working:** (b)(iii) The best answers do not simply reject it. A commercial
source can be authoritative about its own product and unreliable about how well
that product works — the distinction is what earns the second mark.
