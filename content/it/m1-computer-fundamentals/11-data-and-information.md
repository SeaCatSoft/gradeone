## Lesson

In everyday speech, *data* and *information* mean much the same thing. In IT
they do not, and the distinction is examined in almost every paper.

**Data** is raw, unprocessed facts and figures, with no meaning on their own.

**Information** is data that has been processed so that it has meaning and is
useful.

### The difference in one example

Here is data:

```
45   67   88   52   39   71
```

Six numbers. They mean nothing. They could be marks, ages, temperatures or
prices, and there is no way to tell.

Here is information:

> The average mark in the Information Technology test was 60.3, and four of the
> six students passed.

Same numbers. Now they have been processed — averaged, counted, compared
against a pass mark — and given a context. Somebody can now act on them.

The test: **can a decision be made from it?** If not, it is data.

| | Data | Information |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Raw, unprocessed | Processed |
| Meaning | None on its own | Meaningful |
| Useful for decisions | No | Yes |
| Example | `45`, `67`, `88` | "The class average is 60.3" |
| Depends on | Nothing | Data, plus processing and context |

### Processing

**Processing** is what turns one into the other: calculating, sorting,
classifying, summarising, comparing, selecting.

This gives the **data processing cycle**, which is the IPOS cycle wearing
different words:

**Input (collect)** → **Process** → **Output (information)** → **Storage**

Sometimes a further stage is added: **feedback**, where the information
produced influences what is collected next.

### Where data comes from

**Primary source** — data you collect yourself, for your own purpose:
questionnaires, interviews, observation, measurement, experiments.

- Exactly what you need, and you know how it was gathered.
- Costly and slow to collect.

**Secondary source** — data collected by someone else, which you reuse:
published statistics, books, websites, other organisations' reports.

- Cheap and quick to obtain, and may cover far more than you could.
- Gathered for someone else's purpose, so it may not fit yours, may be out of
  date, and may be unreliable — which is the subject of the next lesson.

### Where information goes: knowledge

Some syllabuses extend the chain:

**Data** → **Information** → **Knowledge**

**Knowledge** is information combined with experience and understanding, so it
can be applied. Knowing the class average is information; knowing that this
average is unusually low for this topic, and what to do about it, is knowledge.

### Qualities of good information

Information is only as useful as it is good, and questions ask for these
qualities:

| Quality | Means |
|---|---|
| **Accurate** | Correct, and free from error |
| **Complete** | Nothing important missing |
| **Relevant** | Related to the decision being made |
| **Timely** | Available when it is needed |
| **Concise** | No more than is needed |
| **Understandable** | Presented so the reader can use it |
| **Cost-effective** | Worth more than it cost to produce |

Two of these are worth dwelling on.

**Timely** matters more than people expect. A perfectly accurate sales report
delivered a month after the decision was taken is worthless. Information has a
shelf life.

**GIGO — garbage in, garbage out.** Processing cannot improve bad data. If the
marks entered were wrong, the average is wrong, however carefully it was
calculated, and it will look exactly as convincing as a correct one. This is
the argument for the validation and verification checks in the next lessons.

### Data, information and the computer

A computer accepts **data** as input and produces **information** as output.
That is the whole point of the machine, and it is why the field is called
*information* technology rather than data technology: the data is the raw
material, and the information is the product.

## Flashcards

### 1.11 | Define data.
Raw, unprocessed facts and figures that have no meaning on their own.

### 1.11 | Define information.
Data that has been processed so that it has meaning and is useful for making
decisions.

### 1.11 | What is the test for whether something is information rather than data?
Whether a decision can be made from it. If not, it is data.

### 1.11 | Give an example of data and the information produced from it.
Data: the marks `45, 67, 88, 52`. Information: "The class average is 63 and
three students passed."

### 1.11 | What turns data into information?
**Processing** — calculating, sorting, classifying, summarising, comparing or
selecting.

### 1.11 | State the stages of the data processing cycle.
Input (collection) → processing → output (information) → storage, sometimes
with feedback.

### 1.11 | Distinguish between a primary and a secondary source of data.
**Primary** data is collected by you for your own purpose — a questionnaire or
measurement. **Secondary** data was collected by someone else and is reused.

### 1.11 | Give one advantage and one disadvantage of secondary data.
Advantage: cheap and quick to obtain, and may cover far more than you could
collect. Disadvantage: gathered for someone else's purpose, so it may not fit,
may be out of date, or may be unreliable.

### 1.11 | What is knowledge, in the data–information–knowledge chain?
Information combined with experience and understanding, so that it can be
applied.

### 1.11 | Name five qualities of good information.
Any five: accurate; complete; relevant; timely; concise; understandable;
cost-effective.

### 1.11 | Why does timeliness matter as much as accuracy?
Information has a shelf life. A perfectly accurate report delivered after the
decision was taken is worthless.

### 1.11 | What does GIGO stand for and what does it mean?
**Garbage in, garbage out** — processing cannot improve bad data. Wrong input
produces wrong output, and it looks just as convincing as a correct one.

## Questions

### 1.11 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 1
Which of the following is **data** rather than information?

- A. The average mark was 60.3
- B. `45 67 88 52`
- C. Four of six students passed
- D. Sales rose by 12% this quarter

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The bare numbers have no meaning on their own. The other three
have been processed and given a context, so a decision could be made from them.

### 1.11 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Data becomes information when it is

- A. stored on a disc
- B. processed so that it has meaning
- C. printed on paper
- D. entered into a computer

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Processing is what supplies the meaning. Storing, printing and
entering all leave the data exactly as raw as it was.

### 1.11 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
A student conducts a survey of their own class to find their favourite
subjects. This is an example of

- A. a primary source of data
- B. a secondary source of data
- C. information
- D. knowledge

**Answer:** A

**Working:** The student collected it directly, for their own purpose, which is
what makes it primary. Using published statistics would be secondary.

### 1.11 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
A sales report is completely accurate but is produced three weeks after the
decision it was meant to support. Which quality of good information is
missing?

- A. Accuracy
- B. Relevance
- C. Timeliness
- D. Completeness

**Answer:** C

**Working:** Information must arrive when it is needed. Accuracy does not
rescue a report that comes too late to be used.

### 1.11 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
`GIGO` reminds us that

- A. computers process data faster than people
- B. incorrect input data produces incorrect output, however good the processing
- C. information is more valuable than data
- D. all data should be stored twice

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Garbage in, garbage out. Processing cannot repair bad data, and
the wrong answer looks exactly as convincing as the right one — which is why
data is validated and verified as it is entered.

### 1.11 | structured | 9 marks | difficulty 3
A supermarket records every item sold at its checkouts. At the end of each
week, the manager receives a report showing which products sold best and which
should be reordered.

(a) Distinguish between **data** and **information**, using this supermarket as
your example. **(4 marks)**

(b) Name the process that converts one into the other, and give **two**
examples of what it involves. **(3 marks)**

(c) State **two** qualities the manager's report must have to be useful.
**(2 marks)**

**Answer:** (a) 1 mark — data is raw unprocessed facts with no meaning on their
own; 1 mark — an example, such as the individual records of each item scanned
at the checkout; 1 mark — information is data processed so that it has meaning
and supports decisions; 1 mark — an example, such as the report showing which
products sold best and which need reordering.
(b) 1 mark — processing; 1 mark each for any two: calculating totals; sorting
products by quantity sold; summarising by week; comparing against stock levels;
selecting the products below a reorder level.
(c) 1 mark each, any two: accurate; complete; relevant; timely; concise;
understandable; cost-effective.

**Working:** (a) The four marks split evenly between the two definitions and
two examples drawn from the supermarket. A definition without an example scores
half.

(c) Timeliness is the strongest answer here: a weekly reorder report that
arrives after the shelves are empty has failed whatever else it does.
