## Lesson

There is no such thing as a good computer, only a computer that is good **for
something**. A machine that is ideal for video editing is a waste of money in a
reception area, and one that suits reception cannot edit video at all.

Evaluating a system means comparing what it offers against what the job
requires.

### Start with the requirement

Before looking at any specification, establish what the system must do:

- **What tasks?** Word processing and email, or 3D design and video?
- **How many users**, and at once?
- **What software** must run, and what does that software require?
- **Portable or fixed?**
- **What must it connect to** — printers, a network, specialised equipment?
- **What budget**, including software, training, maintenance and replacement?
- **How long must it last** before it is replaced?

Every specification below is judged against these answers, not in the abstract.

### Reading a specification

| Item | What it tells you | Matters most for |
|---|---|---|
| **Processor** — speed in GHz, number of cores | How fast it calculates, and how many things at once | Video editing, design, simulation, games |
| **RAM** — in GB | How much can be worked on at once | Running several programs, large files |
| **Storage** — capacity, HDD or SSD | How much can be kept, and how fast it loads | Large media collections; SSD for speed |
| **Graphics** — integrated or dedicated card | Handling images and 3D | Design, video, games; irrelevant for office work |
| **Monitor** — size and resolution | How much is visible at once | Design, spreadsheets, programming |
| **Connectivity** — Wi-Fi, Ethernet, Bluetooth, ports | What it can attach to | Networked offices, peripherals |
| **Operating system** | What software will run | Everything |
| **Battery life** | How long away from power | Field and mobile work |
| **Weight and size** | Portability | Travelling users |
| **Expandability** | Whether it can be upgraded | Long service life |
| **Warranty and support** | What happens when it fails | Business use |

### Matching system to purpose

| Purpose | Requires | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Office administration | Modest processor, 8 GB RAM, SSD, reliable keyboard | Word processing and email need little power; the SSD makes it feel fast |
| Video editing | Fast multi-core processor, 32 GB RAM, dedicated graphics, large fast storage | Video is enormous and the work is computation-heavy |
| School laboratory | Reliable desktops, modest specification, networked | Many machines on a budget, easy to repair, no portability needed |
| Sales representative | Laptop, good battery life, light, mobile data | Used away from the office all day |
| Server for a small business | Reliable, redundant discs, plenty of RAM, runs continuously | Many users depend on it; failure stops everyone |
| Graphic design | Dedicated graphics, large high-resolution monitor, lots of RAM | Large images, and colour accuracy on screen |
| Student's first computer | Modest laptop, adequate RAM, SSD | Coursework and research; cost matters most |

### Beyond the hardware

An evaluation that considers only the specification is incomplete, and
examination questions reward the wider view:

**Software compatibility.** Will the software the organisation depends on run
on it? This can rule out an otherwise excellent machine.

**Total cost of ownership**, not just the purchase price: software licences,
training, consumables, maintenance, electricity and eventual replacement.

**Training.** A system nobody can use has failed, however good it is.

**Support and warranty.** For a business, how quickly a fault is repaired may
matter more than how fast the machine is.

**Upgradability.** Can RAM or storage be added later? Laptops and tablets often
cannot, which shortens their useful life.

**Reliability**, especially where downtime is expensive.

**Security.** Does it support the encryption, user accounts and updates the
organisation needs?

**Environmental cost** — power consumption, and what happens to the machine at
the end of its life.

### Over-specifying and under-specifying

Both are failures, and it is worth saying so in an evaluation.

**Under-specified**: the machine cannot do the job, or does it so slowly that
staff waste time and become frustrated. It will also need replacing sooner, so
the saving is temporary.

**Over-specified**: money is spent on capability nobody uses. A dedicated
graphics card in a machine used for email is pure waste — and that money would
have bought more RAM, or two more machines.

The aim is a system that **meets the requirement with a margin for growth**,
not the most powerful system available.

### Writing an evaluation

A good answer does four things:

1. **States the requirement** — what the system is for.
2. **Judges each relevant feature** against that requirement, saying whether it
   is adequate and why.
3. **Identifies what is missing or excessive.**
4. **Reaches a conclusion** — suitable, unsuitable, or suitable with stated
   changes.

The conclusion is what makes it an evaluation rather than a description, and it
is the part most often left out. It must follow from the points made: if two
features fall short, the conclusion cannot be "entirely suitable".

## Flashcards

### 1.9 | Why can no computer be described as simply "good"?
Suitability depends on the purpose. A system ideal for video editing is wasted
in a reception area, and one suited to reception cannot edit video at all.

### 1.9 | Name four questions to ask before evaluating a specification.
Any four: what tasks must it perform; how many users; what software must run;
does it need to be portable; what must it connect to; what is the budget; how
long must it last.

### 1.9 | Which specification matters most for video editing, and why?
A fast multi-core processor with plenty of RAM and dedicated graphics — video
files are enormous and the processing is heavy.

### 1.9 | Why does an SSD make an ordinary office computer feel faster than a faster processor would?
Everyday work is limited by how quickly files and programs load, not by
calculation. An SSD addresses exactly that.

### 1.9 | Why is a dedicated graphics card unnecessary in an office computer?
Word processing and email make no real demands on graphics. The money is better
spent on RAM, an SSD, or another machine.

### 1.9 | What is total cost of ownership?
The full cost of a system over its life — purchase, software licences,
training, consumables, maintenance, electricity and replacement — not just the
purchase price.

### 1.9 | Name four factors beyond the specification that affect suitability.
Any four: software compatibility; total cost of ownership; training; support
and warranty; upgradability; reliability; security; environmental cost.

### 1.9 | What is the consequence of under-specifying a system?
It cannot do the job, or does it slowly enough to waste staff time, and it must
be replaced sooner — so the saving is temporary.

### 1.9 | What is the consequence of over-specifying a system?
Money is spent on capability nobody uses, which could have bought something
that was needed.

### 1.9 | What should a computer system specification aim for?
To meet the requirement with a margin for growth — not to be the most powerful
system available.

### 1.9 | What four things should a written evaluation contain?
The requirement; a judgement of each relevant feature against it; what is
missing or excessive; and a conclusion that follows from those points.

## Questions

### 1.9 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
A company buys computers with dedicated graphics cards for staff who use only
email and word processing. This is an example of

- A. under-specifying
- B. over-specifying
- C. good value
- D. total cost of ownership

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The capability is paid for and never used. That money could have
bought more RAM, faster storage, or additional machines.

### 1.9 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Which specification matters **most** for a computer used to edit high-quality
video?

- A. A large monitor only
- B. A fast multi-core processor with plenty of RAM and dedicated graphics
- C. A long battery life
- D. A modem

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Video editing is one of the most demanding ordinary tasks: large
files, heavy processing, and graphics work throughout.

### 1.9 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Which of the following is part of the **total cost of ownership** but not of
the purchase price?

- A. The processor
- B. Staff training and software licences
- C. The monitor
- D. The keyboard

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Total cost of ownership includes everything over the system's
life — licences, training, maintenance, consumables, power and replacement.

### 1.9 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
A school buys laptops that cannot have RAM added later. The main long-term
consequence is that

- A. they will not run any software
- B. their useful life is shortened, since they cannot be upgraded as demands grow
- C. they cannot connect to a network
- D. they will use more electricity

**Answer:** B

**Working:** A machine that can be upgraded serves longer. One that cannot must
be replaced when its specification is outgrown, which raises the cost over
time.

### 1.9 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Which of the following would make an otherwise excellent computer unsuitable
for a business?

- A. It has an SSD rather than a hard disc
- B. The specialised software the business depends on will not run on its
  operating system
- C. It has more RAM than the business needs
- D. It has a larger monitor than requested

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Software compatibility can rule out a machine whatever its
specification. The other three are either advantages or harmless excess.

### 1.9 | structured | 10 marks | difficulty 3
A small architecture practice needs three computers: one for reception
(appointments, email and letters), one for producing 3D building designs, and
one to act as a server storing all the practice's drawings.

(a) For each computer, state **one** specification that matters most, and give a
reason:

(i) reception **(2 marks)**

(ii) 3D design **(2 marks)**

(iii) the server **(2 marks)**

(b) State **two** costs besides the purchase price that the practice should
consider. **(2 marks)**

(c) Explain why buying the same high-specification computer for all three roles
would be poor value. **(2 marks)**

**Answer:** (a)(i) 1 mark — a modest processor with an SSD (accept: adequate
RAM, a reliable keyboard); 1 mark — the tasks are undemanding, and an SSD makes
programs and files load quickly, which is what the user notices.
(ii) 1 mark — a fast multi-core processor with dedicated graphics and plenty of
RAM; 1 mark — 3D design is computation- and graphics-heavy and works with very
large files.
(iii) 1 mark — large, reliable storage, ideally with redundant discs, and enough
RAM to serve several users; 1 mark — the whole practice depends on it, so
capacity and reliability matter more than speed at any one task.
(b) 1 mark each, any two: software licences; training; maintenance and support;
consumables; electricity; eventual replacement.
(c) 1 mark — the reception machine would be over-specified, paying for graphics
and processing power that is never used; 1 mark — that money could have gone
towards what each role actually needs, such as redundant storage for the server.

**Working:** (a) Each part needs a specification *and* a reason tied to the
role. The three roles are deliberately different: one needs responsiveness, one
needs raw power, and one needs reliability and capacity.

(c) The strongest answers note that a single specification cannot be right for
all three, because the server's real need — redundant storage — is not on the
design machine's list at all.
