## Lesson

The **review** features are the tools for checking a document and for working
on it with other people. They are what turns a draft into something ready to
send.

### Spelling and grammar check

The **spell checker** compares each word against a dictionary and marks
anything it does not recognise, usually with a wavy underline. For each one you
may accept a suggestion, ignore it, or **add to dictionary** — which is what
you do for a correctly spelt name the dictionary has never met.

The **grammar checker** looks for problems of construction: disagreement
between subject and verb, a sentence with no verb, a misplaced comma.

Both are useful and neither is trustworthy. Two failures matter:

**The wrong word, correctly spelt.** `form` for `from`, `their` for `there`,
`principal` for `principle`. Every one is in the dictionary, so nothing is
marked. This is why the checker cannot replace **proofreading** — reading the
document yourself, or better, having someone else read it.

**False alarms.** Names, technical terms and Caribbean place names are flagged
constantly. Accepting every suggestion without reading it introduces errors
rather than removing them.

Check the **language** setting too. A document set to US English marks
`colour` and `organise` as misspellings.

### Thesaurus

The **thesaurus** offers synonyms — alternative words of similar meaning. It is
useful for avoiding repetition, and dangerous used carelessly: synonyms are
rarely exact, and a word chosen because it sounded impressive usually reads as
though it was.

### Word count

**Word count** reports the number of words, characters, paragraphs and pages.
Where a piece of work has a stated limit, this is how you check it.

### Track changes

**Track changes** records every alteration made to a document: insertions,
deletions and formatting changes, each marked with who made it and when.

The original author can then go through them and **accept** or **reject** each
one, individually or all at once. Until a change is accepted, the original text
is still there.

This is how documents are edited collaboratively without anyone losing work. A
supervisor marking up a student's report, a manager revising a colleague's
letter, two people working on a contract — each can see exactly what the other
changed, and can disagree.

Two practical points:

- A document with tracked changes still **contains** the deleted text. Sending
  one to an outsider may reveal edits you did not intend them to see, so
  changes are accepted or rejected before a document goes out.
- Display can usually be switched between showing the markup and showing the
  document as it would look with all changes applied — useful for reading, but
  remember the changes are still only proposed.

### Comments

A **comment** is a note attached to a piece of text without becoming part of
it. It appears in the margin, labelled with its author, and can be **replied
to** and **resolved**.

The distinction from track changes is examined: a **tracked change** alters the
text; a **comment** says something *about* the text and changes nothing. "This
paragraph needs a source" is a comment. Deleting the paragraph is a change.

### Comparing and combining

**Compare** takes two versions of a document and produces a third showing the
differences as tracked changes. It is how you find out what changed when
somebody returned a document without tracking their edits.

**Combine** merges the revisions of several reviewers into one document.

### Other review tools

**Read aloud** reads the document out. It catches missing and repeated words
that the eye slides over.

**Accessibility checker** reports problems for readers using assistive
technology — images with no alternative text, poor colour contrast, tables with
no header row.

**Translate** renders selected text or the whole document into another
language, with the usual warning that machine translation needs checking.

### Getting a document ready

A sensible order before sending anything out:

1. **Spell and grammar check**, reading each suggestion rather than accepting
   blindly.
2. **Proofread** yourself, for the wrong-word errors the checker cannot see.
3. **Check the word count** against any limit.
4. **Resolve all comments**, and delete them.
5. **Accept or reject all tracked changes.**
6. **Run the accessibility checker** if the document will be widely read.
7. **Save as PDF** if it must not be altered.

Steps 4 and 5 are the ones people forget, and they are the ones that embarrass.
A document sent out with a colleague's comment still in the margin is a
familiar and avoidable accident.

## Flashcards

### 4.4 | What does a spell checker do?
Compares each word against a dictionary and marks anything it does not
recognise, offering suggestions.

### 4.4 | Give the main limitation of a spell checker.
It cannot detect a correctly spelt **wrong** word — `form` for `from`, `their`
for `there` — because both are in the dictionary.

### 4.4 | What does "add to dictionary" do?
Stores a word the checker did not recognise — usually a name or technical term
— so it is no longer marked as an error.

### 4.4 | What is the thesaurus used for?
Finding synonyms — words of similar meaning — to avoid repetition. Synonyms are
rarely exact, so the replacement must be read in context.

### 4.4 | What does track changes do?
Records every insertion, deletion and formatting change, with who made it and
when, so each can later be accepted or rejected.

### 4.4 | What is the difference between a tracked change and a comment?
A **tracked change** alters the text. A **comment** is a note *about* the text
and changes nothing.

### 4.4 | Why must tracked changes be accepted or rejected before a document is sent out?
The document still contains the deleted text and a record of who changed what,
which may reveal more than intended.

### 4.4 | What does the Compare feature do?
Takes two versions of a document and produces a third showing the differences
as tracked changes.

### 4.4 | What does an accessibility checker look for?
Problems for readers using assistive technology — images without alternative
text, poor colour contrast, tables without a header row.

### 4.4 | Why check the language setting of a document?
A document set to US English marks British spellings such as `colour` and
`organise` as errors.

### 4.4 | List the review steps before sending a document out.
Spell and grammar check; proofread; check the word count; resolve and delete
comments; accept or reject all tracked changes; run the accessibility checker;
save as PDF if it must not be altered.

## Questions

### 4.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
A spell checker does not flag the sentence "She took the book form the shelf."
This is because

- A. the grammar checker is switched off
- B. `form` is a correctly spelt word found in the dictionary
- C. the sentence contains no errors
- D. the language setting is wrong

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The checker works word by word against a dictionary and cannot
tell that the wrong word was chosen. Only proofreading catches this.

### 4.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Which feature records who made each alteration to a document so the author can
accept or reject them?

- A. Comments
- B. Track changes
- C. Word count
- D. Thesaurus

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Track changes stores each insertion, deletion and formatting
change with its author. Comments annotate the text without altering it.

### 4.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
A teacher wants to suggest that a paragraph needs a source, without altering
the student's text. The teacher should use

- A. track changes
- B. a comment
- C. the thesaurus
- D. find and replace

**Answer:** B

**Working:** A comment says something about the text and changes nothing.
Track changes would be right for proposing an actual edit.

### 4.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Why should tracked changes be accepted or rejected before a report is emailed
to a client?

- A. The file will not open otherwise
- B. The document still contains the deleted text and a record of who changed it
- C. Tracked changes prevent printing
- D. The spell checker cannot run until they are resolved

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Until they are resolved, the original wording and the editing
history travel with the file — which may show the client more than intended.

### 4.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
A document written in Jamaica marks `colour` and `organise` as misspelt. The
most likely cause is that

- A. the words are genuinely misspelt
- B. the document's language is set to US English
- C. track changes is switched on
- D. the thesaurus is open

**Answer:** B

**Working:** A US English dictionary expects `color` and `organize`. Changing
the document's language setting resolves it.

### 4.4 | structured | 9 marks | difficulty 3
A student submits a project to a teacher, who edits it and returns it.

(a) Name the review feature the teacher should use so that the student can see
exactly what was altered and decide whether to keep each alteration.
**(1 mark)**

(b) The teacher also wants to ask a question about one paragraph without
changing it. Name the feature, and state how it differs from your answer to
(a). **(2 marks)**

(c) State **two** limitations of relying on the spell checker alone.
**(2 marks)**

(d) List **four** steps the student should take before submitting the final
version. **(4 marks)**

**Answer:** (a) Track changes — 1 mark.
(b) 1 mark — a comment; 1 mark — a comment is a note about the text and changes
nothing, whereas a tracked change alters the text itself.
(c) 1 mark each, any two: it cannot detect a correctly spelt wrong word such as
`form` for `from`; it flags names and technical terms that are not errors; it
may suggest wrong corrections, so accepting them blindly introduces mistakes;
it does not judge whether the meaning is clear.
(d) 1 mark each, any four: run the spelling and grammar check; proofread the
document personally; check the word count against any limit; resolve and delete
all comments; accept or reject all tracked changes; run the accessibility
checker; save or export as PDF.

**Working:** (b) The second mark is for the distinction, not for describing
comments again. Say what each one does to the text.

(d) Steps 4 and 5 are the ones most often forgotten, and a document submitted
with the teacher's comments still in the margin shows it.
