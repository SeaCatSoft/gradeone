## Lesson

**Automation** is using machines and computers to do work previously done by
people.

The objective says *assess*, and the honest assessment is that automation both
destroys and creates jobs, and that the two do not fall on the same people.
An answer arguing only one side is incomplete.

### Jobs lost

Automation replaces work that is **routine, repetitive and predictable** —
because those are the tasks a machine can be told how to do.

| Job | Replaced by |
|---|---|
| Factory assembly worker | Industrial robots |
| Bank teller | ATMs and online banking |
| Supermarket cashier | Self-service checkouts |
| Typist, filing clerk | Word processors and digital records |
| Telephone operator | Automated exchanges |
| Toll collector | Automatic toll systems |
| Travel agent | Online booking |
| Printer, typesetter | Desktop publishing |
| Warehouse picker | Automated warehouses |

Three further effects, beyond the count of jobs:

**Deskilling.** A job may survive but require less skill, and so pay less. A
skilled craftsman becomes a machine minder.

**Fewer people, more output.** A department of twenty becomes a department of
five doing more work than the twenty did.

**Whole occupations disappear.** Not a reduction but an ending — nobody is
employed as a telephone operator now.

### Jobs created

**New occupations.** Software developers, network administrators, data
analysts, web designers, cybersecurity specialists, robotics engineers, IT
support. None of these existed a lifetime ago, and some did not exist twenty
years ago.

**Building and maintaining the machines.** Robots must be designed, built,
installed, programmed, maintained and repaired.

**Growth creating employment.** Automation lowers costs; lower costs raise
demand; higher demand can require more staff elsewhere in the business —
delivery, sales, support.

**New industries entirely.** Online retail, streaming, mobile applications,
digital marketing, e-sports. Each employs people in roles that did not
previously exist.

**More jobs in what machines cannot do.** Work requiring judgement, creativity,
negotiation, care and physical adaptability — nursing, teaching, plumbing,
counselling, design, repair.

### Why "jobs created" does not settle the argument

This is the part that turns a list into an assessment.

**The jobs are not in the same place.** A factory closing in one town does not
help when the new work is in another.

**They are not for the same people.** A displaced cashier does not become a
data analyst without substantial retraining, which takes time and money and may
not be available. Older workers in particular may not retrain at all.

**The timing is wrong.** The losses come at once, when the machine is
installed. The gains arrive gradually, over years.

**The numbers need not match.** One engineer may maintain the robots that
replaced fifty workers.

**Inequality widens.** Those with technical skills gain; those without lose.
The benefit accrues to capital and skills, and the cost to routine labour.

So the correct assessment is not "automation destroys jobs" nor "automation
creates jobs" but that it **changes** which jobs exist, and imposes real,
concentrated costs on particular people while spreading its benefits more
thinly.

### Which jobs are most at risk

| More at risk | Less at risk |
|---|---|
| Routine and repetitive | Varied and unpredictable |
| Rule-based | Requiring judgement |
| Predictable physical work | Physically adaptable work |
| Data entry and processing | Creative work |
| Little human interaction | Caring and persuading |

Note that this cuts across the old divide between manual and office work. A
data-entry clerk may be at greater risk than an electrician, because the
electrician's work is unpredictable and physically varied, while the clerk's is
neither.

### Effects on those still employed

- **Changed skills** — the job now involves operating and checking a system.
- **Retraining** required, repeatedly, through a career.
- **Job insecurity**, and the stress of expecting to be replaced.
- **Monitoring** — automated systems record productivity in detail.
- **Flexibility**: remote work and flexible hours, made possible by the same
  technology.
- **Safer work**, where machines take on the dangerous and physically damaging
  tasks. This is a genuine benefit and is often left out of answers.

### Responses

**Education and retraining** — publicly funded, available throughout a career
rather than only at its start.

**Digital skills in schools**, so the next generation is not displaced by the
same change.

**Redeployment** within an organisation rather than redundancy.

**Notice and support** where jobs are lost.

**Encouraging new industries** so that displaced workers have somewhere to go.

### In a Caribbean context

Two effects pull in opposite directions.

**Against**: economies with large tourism, manufacturing, agricultural and
clerical sectors have many routine roles exposed to automation, and less
capacity to fund large-scale retraining.

**For**: the same technology allows people to work for employers anywhere
without leaving the region — software development, support, design and other
remote services. This can retain skilled people who would otherwise emigrate,
provided connectivity and training are available.

Which effect dominates is not a matter of technology but of what is invested in
education and infrastructure — and saying that is exactly the assessment the
objective is asking for.

## Flashcards

### 3.4 | Define automation.
Using machines and computers to do work previously done by people.

### 3.4 | Which kinds of job are most at risk from automation?
Routine, repetitive, predictable and rule-based work, especially with little
human interaction.

### 3.4 | Which kinds of job are least at risk?
Work requiring judgement, creativity, negotiation, care, or physical
adaptability — nursing, teaching, plumbing, design, repair.

### 3.4 | Name four jobs largely replaced by automation.
Any four: bank teller; supermarket cashier; typist or filing clerk; telephone
operator; toll collector; travel agent; factory assembly worker; typesetter.

### 3.4 | What is deskilling?
A job surviving automation but requiring less skill, and so paying less — a
skilled craftsman becoming a machine minder.

### 3.4 | Name four jobs created by computerisation.
Any four: software developer; network administrator; data analyst; web
designer; cybersecurity specialist; robotics engineer; IT support technician.

### 3.4 | Give three reasons why "automation creates jobs" does not fully answer the objection.
Any three: the new jobs are in different places; they need different skills, so
displaced workers cannot simply take them; the losses come at once while the
gains arrive slowly; the numbers need not match.

### 3.4 | Why does automation tend to widen inequality?
Those with technical skills gain from it, while those doing routine work bear
the cost. The benefit goes to capital and skills.

### 3.4 | Why might a data-entry clerk be at greater risk than an electrician?
Automation replaces predictable rule-based work. The electrician's work is
varied, unpredictable and physically adaptable; the clerk's is none of these.

### 3.4 | Give one benefit of automation for workers who keep their jobs.
Dangerous and physically damaging tasks are taken over by machines, making the
work safer. (Also: flexible and remote working.)

### 3.4 | Name three responses to job losses from automation.
Any three: publicly funded retraining available throughout a career; digital
skills taught in schools; redeployment within the organisation; notice and
support for those losing jobs; encouraging new industries.

### 3.4 | State the balanced assessment of automation's effect on employment.
It does not simply destroy or create jobs — it **changes** which jobs exist,
imposing concentrated costs on particular workers while spreading its benefits
more thinly.

> Hint: the losses and the gains do not fall on the same people.

## Questions

### 3.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Which job is **most** at risk from automation?

- A. A nurse
- B. A data-entry clerk
- C. A plumber
- D. A teacher

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Data entry is routine, rule-based and predictable — exactly what a
machine can be told how to do. The others need judgement, care or physical
adaptability.

### 3.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Which of the following is a job **created** by computerisation?

- A. Telephone operator
- B. Typesetter
- C. Network administrator
- D. Toll collector

**Answer:** C

**Working:** The other three have largely disappeared through automation.
Network administration did not exist before computer networks did.

### 3.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
A factory installs robots and the workforce falls from 60 to 12, while 3
maintenance engineers are hired. Which statement best assesses this?

- A. No jobs were lost, since new jobs were created
- B. Jobs were both lost and created, but not in equal numbers or for the same
  people
- C. Automation always increases total employment
- D. The displaced workers will automatically become engineers

**Answer:** B

**Working:** 48 jobs went and 3 appeared, and the new roles need skills the
displaced workers do not have. A balanced assessment states both halves and the
mismatch between them.

### 3.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
What is meant by **deskilling**?

- A. Workers refusing to learn new skills
- B. A job surviving automation but requiring less skill and paying less
- C. Removing a job entirely
- D. Training workers in digital skills

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The job remains, but the skill it demanded has been transferred to
the machine, leaving a less skilled and lower-paid role.

### 3.4 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Which is a genuine benefit of automation for those who remain employed?

- A. Greater job insecurity
- B. More detailed monitoring of their productivity
- C. Dangerous tasks being taken over by machines
- D. The need for repeated retraining

**Answer:** C

**Working:** Machines take on the hazardous and physically damaging work, which
makes jobs safer. The other three are genuine effects, but they are costs
rather than benefits.

### 3.4 | structured | 10 marks | difficulty 3
A large supermarket chain replaces 40 staffed checkouts with self-service
machines across its stores.

(a) State **two** effects on the employees. **(2 marks)**

(b) Describe **two** ways the same technology creates employment. **(4 marks)**

(c) Explain why the creation of new jobs does not fully compensate the
displaced cashiers. **(2 marks)**

(d) State **two** measures that could reduce the harm to those affected.
**(2 marks)**

**Answer:** (a) 1 mark each, any two: cashiers lose their jobs; remaining staff
supervise several machines rather than operating one till, so the role changes;
deskilling and possibly lower pay; job insecurity among those who remain.
(b) 2 marks each for two developed points: the machines must be designed,
manufactured, installed, maintained and repaired, creating technical jobs; the
software must be written and supported; lower costs may allow the chain to open
more stores, employing staff in other roles; new roles appear supervising
self-service areas and helping customers.
(c) 1 mark — the new jobs need different skills, so a cashier cannot take one
without substantial retraining; 1 mark — the new roles are fewer, may be
elsewhere, and appear gradually while the job losses happen at once.
(d) 1 mark each, any two: retraining programmes; redeployment within the
company to other roles; adequate notice and redundancy support; teaching
digital skills in schools; government support for new industries.

**Working:** (c) carries the assessment. The strongest answers name the
mismatch in **skills**, in **numbers**, in **place** and in **timing** — the
losses are immediate and concentrated, the gains slow and dispersed.
