## Lesson

**Mobile communication** lets people send and receive data while moving, with
no physical connection to anything. The objective asks you to **assess its
importance**, which means arguing a case rather than listing devices.

### The technologies

**Cellular networks.** The area is divided into **cells**, each served by a
base station (a mast). A phone communicates with the nearest one, and as the
user moves, the call is **handed over** to the next cell without interruption.
Dividing the area this way lets the same radio frequencies be reused in
different cells, which is what allows millions of users to share a limited
range of frequencies.

The generations:

| Generation | Brought |
|---|---|
| **1G** | Analogue voice only |
| **2G** | Digital voice, and text messages (SMS) |
| **3G** | Mobile internet at usable speeds |
| **4G** | Fast broadband — video streaming, video calls |
| **5G** | Much higher speed, very low delay, many devices at once |

**Wi-Fi.** Wireless access to a local network, typically within a building.
Faster than cellular and free to the user once installed, but limited in range.

**Bluetooth.** Short-range — a few metres — and low power, for connecting
devices to each other: earphones, keyboards, car audio, fitness bands.

**NFC** — near field communication. A few centimetres, used for contactless
payment and travel cards. The tiny range is a **security feature**: a card must
be presented deliberately.

**GPS** — the global positioning system. A receiver works out its position from
signals sent by satellites. It underlies navigation, delivery tracking, ride
services, mapping and location-tagged photographs.

**Satellite communication.** Reaches places with no other coverage, which
matters for shipping, aviation, remote communities and disaster response.

**Mobile broadband and tethering.** A phone's cellular connection shared with a
laptop.

### Why mobile matters — the assessment

**Access where there is no fixed infrastructure.** This is the strongest
argument, and the most important in the Caribbean and other developing regions.
Laying cable to every home is slow and expensive; erecting masts is far
cheaper. Many communities reached mobile telephony and mobile internet without
ever having had a fixed line, skipping a generation of technology entirely.

**The phone as the only computer.** For a large share of the world's people,
a smartphone is the only computer they own and their only route to the
internet. Any service that assumes a laptop excludes them — which is why
"design for mobile" is a practical necessity rather than a preference.

**Mobile money.** Where few people have bank accounts but most have phones,
money can be sent, received and stored by phone. This has brought financial
services to people whom banks never reached.

**Commerce.** Mobile banking, mobile payment, online shopping and market-price
information for farmers and fishers, who can now find out what their catch or
crop is worth before selling it.

**Work.** People can work away from an office, respond immediately, and
coordinate from anywhere — which changed both what work is possible and where
it is done.

**Education.** Lessons, reference material and courses reach students far from
a school or library.

**Health.** Remote consultation, reminders to take medicine, and reporting from
places with no clinic.

**Emergencies.** Warnings reach people directly, and people in difficulty can
call for help. After a hurricane, mobile networks are usually restored long
before fixed lines, because a mast can be repaired or replaced far faster than
a cable network.

**Social connection.** Messaging and video calls keep families in touch across
countries — significant in the Caribbean, where many families have members
living abroad.

**The Internet of Things.** Devices that are not phones — meters, vehicles,
sensors, trackers — communicate over mobile networks, which is why 5G's ability
to support enormous numbers of connections matters.

### The costs

An assessment names both sides:

- **Cost.** Data and devices are expensive relative to income in many places,
  so a divide remains between those who can afford them and those who cannot.
- **Coverage gaps** persist in rural and mountainous areas.
- **Security.** Wireless signals can be intercepted; public Wi-Fi is often
  unencrypted; a lost phone may expose everything on it.
- **Privacy.** A phone knows where it is at all times, and that information is
  recorded by someone.
- **Distraction and overuse**, including the expectation of being always
  available.
- **Health and safety** — using a phone while driving.
- **Dependence.** When the network fails, people who rely on it entirely have
  no alternative left.
- **Electronic waste** from devices replaced every few years.

### Reaching a judgement

The objective says *assess*, so an answer needs a conclusion.

A defensible one: mobile communication is now the **primary** means of
connection for most of the world's people rather than a supplement to fixed
networks, and in regions where fixed infrastructure was never built it is the
only one there has ever been. Its drawbacks are real, but they are arguments
for addressing cost, coverage and security — not for regarding it as optional.

## Flashcards

### 2.3 | Why is a cellular network divided into cells?
So the same radio frequencies can be reused in different cells, allowing
millions of users to share a limited range of frequencies.

### 2.3 | What is handover in a cellular network?
Passing a call from one cell's base station to the next as the user moves, so
the connection is not interrupted.

### 2.3 | What did each of 2G, 3G and 4G bring?
**2G** digital voice and text messaging; **3G** mobile internet at usable
speeds; **4G** fast broadband, making video streaming and video calls
practical.

### 2.3 | What does 5G add beyond 4G?
Much higher speed, very low delay, and support for an enormous number of
connected devices at once.

### 2.3 | Distinguish between Bluetooth and NFC by range and use.
**Bluetooth**: a few metres, for connecting devices such as earphones and
keyboards. **NFC**: a few centimetres, for contactless payment and travel
cards.

### 2.3 | Why is NFC's very short range a security advantage?
The card or phone must be presented deliberately and close, so it cannot be
read from a distance.

### 2.3 | How does GPS work, and name three uses.
A receiver calculates its position from signals sent by satellites. Used for
navigation, delivery tracking, ride services, mapping and photo location.

### 2.3 | Why has mobile communication been especially important in developing regions?
Erecting masts is far cheaper and faster than laying cable to every home, so
many communities gained mobile telephony and internet without ever having had a
fixed line.

### 2.3 | What is mobile money and why does it matter?
Sending, receiving and storing money by phone. Where few people have bank
accounts but most have phones, it brings financial services to people banks
never reached.

### 2.3 | Why are mobile networks important after a natural disaster?
They are usually restored long before fixed lines, because a mast can be
repaired or replaced far faster than a cable network.

### 2.3 | Give four drawbacks of mobile communication technologies.
Any four: the cost of data and devices; gaps in coverage; security risks from
interception and lost devices; privacy, since a phone records its location;
distraction and overuse; danger when used while driving; dependence;
electronic waste.

### 2.3 | Why does "design for mobile" matter practically rather than aesthetically?
For a large share of the world's people a phone is the only computer they own,
so a service that assumes a laptop excludes them.

## Questions

### 2.3 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Cellular networks divide an area into cells mainly so that

- A. each cell can use a different protocol
- B. the same frequencies can be reused in different cells
- C. calls cost less
- D. phones do not need batteries

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Frequency reuse is what allows a limited range of radio
frequencies to serve millions of users at once.

### 2.3 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Which technology has a range of only a few centimetres and is used for
contactless payment?

- A. Bluetooth
- B. Wi-Fi
- C. NFC
- D. GPS

**Answer:** C

**Working:** Near field communication works over a few centimetres, and that
short range is deliberate — the card must be presented on purpose.

### 2.3 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Which generation of mobile network first made mobile internet available at
usable speeds?

- A. 1G
- B. 2G
- C. 3G
- D. 5G

**Answer:** C

**Working:** 1G was analogue voice, 2G added digital voice and text, and 3G
brought the mobile internet.

### 2.3 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Why has mobile telephony spread faster than fixed-line telephony in many
developing countries?

- A. Mobile phones are more powerful than computers
- B. Erecting masts is far cheaper and quicker than laying cable to every home
- C. Fixed lines cannot carry voice
- D. Mobile networks need no infrastructure at all

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The cost of infrastructure is the deciding factor. Many
communities gained mobile service without ever having had a fixed line, skipping
a generation of technology.

### 2.3 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 3
Which is a genuine drawback of widespread mobile communication?

- A. It reaches remote communities
- B. A phone records its location continuously, raising privacy concerns
- C. It allows people to work away from an office
- D. It restores service quickly after a hurricane

**Answer:** B

**Working:** The other three are advantages. Continuous location recording is
one of the real costs, along with security, expense and dependence.

### 2.3 | structured | 10 marks | difficulty 3
A Caribbean island has limited fixed-line telephone infrastructure, but mobile
coverage reaches most of the population.

(a) Explain **two** reasons why mobile communication has become more important
than fixed-line communication in such a country. **(4 marks)**

(b) Describe **two** ways mobile technology benefits ordinary people there.
**(4 marks)**

(c) State **two** drawbacks that remain. **(2 marks)**

**Answer:** (a) 2 marks each for two developed reasons: erecting masts is far
cheaper and faster than laying cable to every household, so coverage was
achieved where fixed lines never were; for many people a phone is the only
computer they own, so it is their only route to the internet; mobile networks
are restored far more quickly after a hurricane than cable networks.
(b) 2 marks each for two developed benefits: mobile money lets people send,
receive and store money without a bank account; farmers and fishers can check
market prices before selling; students can reach lessons and reference material
far from a school; remote health consultation and medicine reminders; families
with members living abroad stay in contact; emergency warnings reach people
directly.
(c) 1 mark each, any two: data and devices remain expensive relative to income;
coverage gaps persist in rural or mountainous areas; security risks from
interception or a lost phone; privacy concerns over location data; dependence
on a network that can fail; electronic waste.

**Working:** (a) and (b) each want the point **and** its development. "Mobile
is cheaper" earns one mark; explaining that masts cost far less than cabling
every household, so coverage reached places fixed lines never did, earns both.

(c) These carry one mark each and need no development, but they must be
genuine drawbacks rather than restatements of the benefits.
