## Lesson

**Statistics** is about collecting, organising and interpreting data to answer
questions.

### Population and sample

- The **population** is the whole group you want to know about: every student
  in a school, every voter in a country, every light bulb a factory makes.
- A **sample** is the part of the population you actually collect data from.

We usually sample because checking the whole population is too slow, too
expensive, or impossible (you can't test every light bulb to destruction and
still have bulbs to sell).

A value that describes a **population** is a **parameter**; the matching value
worked out from a **sample** is a **statistic**. The average height of all
students in the school is a parameter; the average height of the 50 you
measured is a statistic, used to **estimate** the parameter.

### A good sample

A sample is only useful if it **represents** the population. It should be:

- **large enough** — a sample of 3 tells you little
- **random** — every member of the population has an equal chance of being
  chosen

Asking only your friends, or only people at the gym, gives a **biased**
sample: it leans towards one group.

### Types of data

**Qualitative** (categorical) data describes a quality with words: favourite
colour, type of car, blood group.

**Quantitative** (numerical) data is counted or measured, and comes in two
kinds:

| Discrete | Continuous |
|---|---|
| can only take certain separate values, usually by **counting** | can take **any** value in a range, by **measuring** |
| number of siblings, goals scored, shoe size | height, mass, time, temperature |

A quick test: can it sensibly be 2.37? If not, it's discrete. Shoe sizes come
in halves but not in between, so they're discrete even though they aren't
whole numbers.

### Levels of measurement

Data can also be classified by what you can meaningfully do with it:

| Level | What it allows | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | names or labels only; no order | eye colour, bus route |
| **Ordinal** | a meaningful order, but gaps between aren't equal | grades A–E, "poor / fair / good" |
| **Interval** | equal gaps, but no true zero | temperature in °C (0 °C isn't "no heat") |
| **Ratio** | equal gaps **and** a true zero, so ratios make sense | mass, height, income |

With ratio data, "twice as much" means something: 40 kg is twice 20 kg. With
interval data it doesn't: 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C.

## Flashcards

### 1.1 | What is the difference between a population and a sample?
The **population** is the whole group being studied. A **sample** is the part
of it that data is actually collected from.

### 1.1 | Why do we use samples instead of whole populations?
Studying everyone is often too slow, too costly or impossible.

### 1.1 | Is the number of pets a family owns discrete or continuous?
**Discrete.** It's counted, and can't be 2.5.

### 1.1 | Is the time taken to run 100 m discrete or continuous?
**Continuous.** It's measured and can take any value in a range.

### 1.1 | Which level of measurement are exam grades A, B, C?
**Ordinal.** They have an order, but the gaps between them aren't equal.

### 1.1 | What makes a sample biased?
It over-represents part of the population, so it doesn't reflect the whole.

## Questions

### 1.1 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 1
Which of these is **continuous** data?

- A. The number of students in a class
- B. The masses of new-born babies
- C. The colours of cars in a car park
- D. The number of goals in a match

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Mass is measured and can take any value in a range. A and D are
counted (discrete), and C is qualitative.

### 1.1 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
A survey about school meals asks only the students in the lunch queue. Why
might the results be unreliable?

- A. The sample is too large.
- B. The sample is biased: students who don't buy lunch are left out.
- C. Lunch data is continuous.
- D. It uses the whole population.

**Answer:** B

**Working:** Everyone in the queue has chosen to buy school lunch, so their
views won't represent students who don't.

### 1.1 | mcq | 1 mark | difficulty 2
Temperature measured in °C is an example of which level of measurement?

- A. Nominal
- B. Ordinal
- C. Interval
- D. Ratio

**Answer:** C

**Working:** The gaps between degrees are equal, but 0 °C isn't a true zero
("no temperature"), so it's interval, not ratio.

### 1.1 | structured | 4 marks | difficulty 2
A head teacher wants to know the average time students in a school of 900
spend on homework each night. She asks 60 students.

(a) Identify the population and the sample. **(2 marks)**

(b) Is "time spent on homework" discrete or continuous? **(1 mark)**

(c) Suggest one way to make sure the sample is representative. **(1 mark)**

**Answer:** (a) Population: all 900 students. Sample: the 60 students asked.
(b) Continuous (c) Choose the 60 at random, for example by drawing names from
the full school list.

**Working:** (c) Other good answers: include students from every year group in
proportion to its size.
